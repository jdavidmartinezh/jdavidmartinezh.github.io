Adaptive Prediction Intervals for Individual Treatment Effects

Paper

J. David Martínez Hernández

Working paper · November 2026

Conformal prediction can construct finite-sample-valid prediction intervals for individual treatment effects, but standard procedures apply the same margin of error to everyone. I propose an adaptive procedure that estimates separate margins for groups with similar predictability and selects among candidate groupings by average interval width. In simulations, average interval width falls by roughly one quarter to one third relative to one margin for everyone; in three of four empirical applications, gains concentrate among individuals whose outcomes are easiest to predict.

Full abstract

Conformal prediction uses held-out prediction errors to turn any model’s predictions into prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees under weak distributional assumptions. These guarantees make it attractive for constructing prediction sets for individual treatment effects (ITEs) without point identification. However, popular conformal procedures for ITEs apply the same margin of error to everyone, potentially producing intervals that are too narrow for individuals whose outcomes are hard to predict and unnecessarily wide for those whose outcomes are easier to predict. I propose an adaptive procedure that instead estimates separate margins for groups of individuals whose outcomes are similarly predictable. Grouping can reduce average interval width by lowering margins for more predictable outcomes, but finer groups leave fewer observations to estimate each margin. I therefore compare candidate groupings fixed before calibration and select the one with the smallest average interval width. To account for a potential winner’s curse, I derive lower confidence bounds on group coverage that remain valid after selection. In simulations, reductions in average interval width range from a quarter to a third relative to one margin for everyone. In three of four empirical applications, gains concentrate among individuals whose outcomes are easiest to predict.

Working Papers

Foreign Propaganda, Trust, and Public Policy: Evidence from Russian Misinformation in the Baltics

Paper

Horacio Larreguy , Jesús David Martínez Hernández

Working paper · August 2026

We study whether Russian state-sponsored misinformation and propaganda alter consequential behavior and political attitudes in former Soviet states. Using disruptions to Western COVID-19 vaccines and data from Latvia, we show that pro-Kremlin media traffic rose and Moderna/Pfizer vaccination fell more in municipalities with larger ethnic Russian shares after the events, patterns that reversed after Russia's invasion of Ukraine and Latvia's restrictions on Russian state-backed media. Among the Baltic states, Latvia is the only country where we find these patterns, consistent with its relatively more difficult history of integrating ethnic Russians and the Russian language into its media space.

Full abstract

We study whether Russian state-sponsored misinformation and propaganda alter consequential behavior and political attitudes in former Soviet states. We exploit two salient disruptions to Western COVID-19 vaccines, the temporary suspension of AstraZeneca and the delayed Janssen rollout, that Russian state-backed media used to promote Sputnik V and discredit Western institutions. Combining online traffic, administrative vaccination records, survey data, and municipal ethnic composition, we estimate differential responses across Latvian municipalities. Following each vaccine-safety event, traffic shifts toward pro-Kremlin Russian-language outlets and Moderna/Pfizer vaccination falls more in municipalities with larger ethnic Russian shares. Both patterns reverse after Russia's invasion of Ukraine and Latvia's restrictions on Russian state-backed media. The vaccination effects are concentrated in first doses and cohorts partly schooled under Soviet rule, and we find no analogous pattern in Estonia or Lithuania. Eurobarometer estimates also show a relative worsening in attitudes toward Latvian democracy and the EU among Russian-language respondents following the vaccine-safety events.

Coercive Capacity, Social Dissent, Repression or Redistribution? Evidence from Authoritarian Mexico

Paper

Horacio Larreguy , Jesús David Martínez Hernández, Juan Felipe Riaño , Mariano Sánchez-Talanquer

Working paper · April 2026

We study how inherited coercive capacity shapes authoritarian responses to rising dissent. Using newly digitized records of repression, we show that after a sharp rise in social dissent in the mid-1960s, Mexican municipalities with stronger militia presence experienced more repression and less land redistribution.

Full abstract

Why do authoritarian regimes exhibit subnational variation in their use of repression versus redistribution to manage local dissent? We develop a model in which an autocrat's localized response to dissent depends on incomplete information about the geography of people's discontent and the government's coercive capacity, a feature that authoritarian governments often inherit from the past. The model predicts that when dissent rises, rulers are more likely to rely on repression rather than redistribution in areas with strong inherited coercive capacity. We then test these predictions using novel data from twentieth-century Mexico under PRI rule. Our empirical strategy exploits a sharp increase in overall dissent in the mid-1960s and three sources of subnational variation: (1) a large-scale land reform that redistributed over half of all agricultural land between 1910 and 1992; (2) the localized presence of regime-aligned rural militias with revolutionary origins; and (3) newly digitized archival records documenting individual instances of repression over time. Difference-in-Differences and Instrumental Variable estimates indicate that after the sudden increase in dissent, municipalities with a stronger militia presence experienced more repression events and less land redistribution than areas with weaker or no militia presence. Our findings demonstrate how historical variation in inherited coercive capacity and informational frictions can shape the subnational logic of authoritarian control.
